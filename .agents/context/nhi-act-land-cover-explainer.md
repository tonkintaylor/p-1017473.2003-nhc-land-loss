## What NHCover insures
- NHCover applies to residential buildings and residential land for physical loss or damage that is the direct result of a natural hazard.
- Buildings are insured for replacement cost (up to the building cover cap); land is insured on an indemnity basis (assessed market value of the damaged land, plus undepreciated value of damaged land structures).
- There is no land cover without a fire insurance contract in force for the residential building.

## The residential building drives land cover
- 'Residential building' = the whole eligible building containing one or more dwellings, plus appurtenant structures for those dwellings, plus service infrastructure for those dwellings.
- If there are two residential buildings on the site (each containing a dwelling), insured land is worked out separately for each, and there are two land cover caps.
- A self-contained sleepout may be a separate residential building (own cap) rather than an appurtenant structure (no separate land cap).

## Imminent damage
- Imminent damage counts as damage. Insured land areas and insured land structures where loss or damage is imminent are included in the assessment and settlement.
- Physical loss or damage that has not yet occurred is imminent damage if:
  - a natural hazard has occurred; and
  - the Commission is of the opinion that, as a direct result of that event, the loss or damage is more likely than not to occur within 12 months after it first occurred.
- Where imminent damage is established, the claim entitlement must factor in the cost to either:
  - prevent the imminent damage occurring (mitigation cost), or
  - repair the imminent damage once it occurs (future reinstatement cost for land claims).
- What estimate must assume normal weather patterns, with no extraordinary events; and no remediation or mitigation of the original natural hazard damage

## Areas of insured land (the 8m/60m rules)

Insured land areas must sit within the insured person's land (the land holding - freehold title plus easements; excludes licences to occupy).

Insured land areas are:
- the land the residential building is situated on (excluding service infrastructure)
- all land within 8m, in a horizontal line, of the building
- land within 60m, in a horizontal line, that is the main access way from the boundary of the insured person's land, or land supporting that access way

Insured land structures are:
- retaining walls and their support systems within 60m of the building, necessary to support or protect the building or the insured land areas this can be inside the insured person's land, or outside it where the insured person has an insurable interest.
- bridges and culverts situated on or in the insured land areas (i.e. under the building, within 8m, or within 60m as part of the main access way). The whole structure must qualify to be covered - a bridge partly beyond 8m is not insured unless it qualifies via the access way route.

## Land associated with appurtenant structures
- A structure is an appurtenant structure if it is part of the eligible building (but not the dwelling) or is a separate building or other immovable structure (e.g. garage, shed); is appurtenant to the dwelling; and is used for household purposes, for access, or to house service infrastructure for the dwelling.
- 'Immovable' is judged on whether the structure can be moved without disassembly, shoring, damage, or heavy machinery - being liftable by crane doesn't make it movable.
- Appurtenant structures attract land cover, like the building does - retaining walls are measured within 60m of appurtenant structures, where necessary to support or protect either the structure, or the insured land areas.
- There is no separate land cover cap for an appurtenant structure - it extends the insured footprint but not the cap count.
- There is no land cover for excluded property (set out in Schedule 2, e.g. fences, letterboxes, clotheslines, chicken coops, tanks, swimming pools etc.)

## Valuation
- All land is valued on an indemnity basis.
- Assessed market value is the prior market value, i.e. the market value immediately before the damage occurred, including GST. Valuations are as at the date of loss.
- Market value is of the bare land (no improvements), assessed at highest and best use, using a direct sales comparison approach under IVS 104.
- Area cap = the lesser of the district plan minimum area and 4,000 m²; if there is no district plan minimum, it is 4,000 m².
  - If the damaged area ≤ area cap → value the actual damaged area.
  - If the damaged area > area cap → value a hypothetical area equal to the area cap, in the same place, with the same features.
- Utility approach is where different rates are used for different areas of land based on their utility, summing to the whole-of-land value. Generally, land under the building is worth most, then land immediately around it, then access, then wider yard; north/west and sunny/view land rates higher.
- Diminution of value (DOV) applies where reinstatement isn't technically feasible, can't lawfully be carried out, or is disproportionately expensive. DOV excludes building damage, damage to other land, general stigma, market movements and regulatory change.
- Valuation types: standard (on-site or desktop) vs alternative (adjusted rating valuation, notional valuation). In practice, alternative valuations are not used for landslide damage, imminent damage to land areas, actual or imminent damage to land structures, shared land, sites >4,000 m², variable-utility sites, or complex ownership. Desktop is out where reinstatement cost is ≥120% of the land cover cap.

## Sub-caps for land structures
- The maximum cap for land structures is (applicable limit):
  - For retaining walls - the number of dwellings × $50,000 + GST
  - For bridges/culverts - dwellings × $25,000 + GST
- Two costings are completed for land structures, both by assessors:
  - Reinstatement/repair cost (the scope of works): what it will actually cost to fix the damage. This reflects the real remedial solution, including demolition, enabling works, site access and compliance with current building standards.
  - Undepreciated value - what it would have cost to build that same wall when it was new. Costed across the whole insured wall even if only part is damaged, with no deduction for age, and excluding demolition, enabling works, site access and any upgrade to current standards.

### How this fits into the land cover cap

Where insured land structures are damaged, that component of the land cover cap is the lesser of the undepreciated value and the applicable limit ($50,000 + GST for retaining walls; $25,000 + GST for bridges/culverts).

The land cover cap is the sum of:
- the assessed market value of the damaged insured land areas, plus
- for damaged retaining walls, the lesser of their undepreciated value and number of dwellings in the residential building × $50,000 plus GST, plus
- for damaged bridges and/or culverts, the lesser of their undepreciated value and number of dwellings × $25,000 plus GST.

| Point | Rule |
|---|---|
| Multiplier | Number of dwellings in the residential building (not the number of walls or owners) |
| Retaining walls | $50,000 + GST per dwelling → 1 dwelling = $57,500 5 dwellings = $250,000 + GST |
| Bridges and culverts | $25,000 + GST per dwelling |
| Undamaged/uninsured structures | Not included in calculation |
| Entitlement | The lesser of cost to repair and the land cover cap |
| Land excess | $500 per dwelling, capped at $5,000 |

### Example 1

A single residential building containing one dwelling. An earthquake causes substantial cracking to a retaining wall within 60 m of the building that supports the insured land areas. No damage to the insured land areas themselves.

The three figures needed to determine settlement:

| Figure | Amount |
|---|---|
| Repair cost (scope of works) | $120,000 |
| Undepreciated value of the wall | $70,000 |
| Applicable sub-cap limit for RTW (1 dwelling × $50,000 + GST) | $57,500 |

The land cover cap: $57,500 (because is it less than the undepreciated value)

Settlement: $57,500 less excess (because it is less than the repair cost)

### Example 2

Same single dwelling. A landslide damages 60 m² of insured land and the retaining wall. The damaged area is under the area cap (say the minimum-sized site is 500 m²), so we value the actual damaged area.

| Component | Figure |
|---|---|
| Assessed market value of the 60 m² damaged land | $45,000 (market value) |
| Undepreciated value of the wall (what it would have cost to build that same wall when it was new) | $70,000 (exceeds the applicable NHI Act sub limit) |
| Applicable sub cap limit for RTW | $57,500 (NHI Act sub cap limit) |
| Total repair cost (land + wall) | $210,000 (SOW) |

The land cover cap (the most we can pay up) = $102,500 ($45k + $57,500)

Cost to repair = $210,000

Settlement is on the land cover cap (less excess) as it is less than cost to repair

### Example 3

Using the same facts above, but the wall is a modest timber pole wall.

| Component | Figure |
|---|---|
| Assessed market value of damaged land | $45,000 |
| Undepreciated value of the wall (what it would have cost to build that same wall when it was new) | $30,000 |
| Applicable sub cap limit for RTW | $57,500 |
| Total repair cost (land + wall) | $58,000 |

Land cover cap = $75,000 ($45,000 + $30,00)

The sub-cap is irrelevant here, because $30,000 undepreciated value is less.

Cost to repair = $58,000

Settlement is on the cost to repair (less excess) because it is lesser than the land cover cap.
